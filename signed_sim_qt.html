Theory — Signed Binary Multipliers
What is Booth's Algorithm?
Booth's algorithm is a signed binary multiplication method that works natively in two's complement representation. Unlike unsigned multipliers that only add, Booth's algorithm can both add and subtract — which allows it to handle negative numbers correctly without any special pre-processing.
Two's complement — how signs work

In two's complement, the leftmost bit is the sign bit. A 0 means positive, a 1 means negative. To negate a number, flip all its bits and add 1. For example, +5 in 4-bit is 0101. Flip → 1010, add 1 → 1011 = −5. The product of two n-bit signed numbers needs 2n bits to hold the full range without overflow.

The core insight

Booth noticed that long runs of 1s in the multiplier are wasteful — each 1 causes an addition. He replaced each run of 1s with one subtraction at the start and one addition at the end, reducing the number of operations. The algorithm inspects consecutive bit pairs to decide what to do at each cycle.

Bit-pair decision table
Encoding Rules
Q₀ Q₋₁ = 00 → No operation, just shift
Q₀ Q₋₁ = 01 → ADD multiplicand to ACC
Q₀ Q₋₁ = 10 → SUBTRACT multiplicand from ACC
Q₀ Q₋₁ = 11 → No operation, just shift
Step-by-step process
1
Load registers
Place multiplicand A into register M. Place multiplier B into register Q. Set accumulator ACC = 0 and appended bit Q₋₁ = 0.
2
Inspect bit pair (Q₀, Q₋₁)
Read the least significant bit of Q (called Q₀) and Q₋₁. Apply the encoding rule above — add M to ACC, subtract M from ACC, or do nothing.
3
Arithmetic right shift
Shift the combined ACC|Q register one position to the right. This is an arithmetic shift — the sign bit of ACC is preserved (replicated), not filled with zero. Q₋₁ takes the old Q₀ value.
4
Repeat n times
Steps 2 and 3 run exactly n times, where n is the bit width. No early exit — every cycle runs.
5
Read the result
The final 2n-bit product is the concatenation of ACC (upper half) and Q (lower half), interpreted as a two's complement signed integer.
Worked example — 5 × (−3)
4-bit example: A = 0101 (+5), B = 1101 (−3)

Quantum Signed Multiplication
The quantum approach to signed multiplication separates the problem into two independent parts — sign determination and magnitude multiplication — and solves them in parallel using superposition, achieving O(log n) circuit depth.
The sign-magnitude strategy

Any signed multiplication A × B can be decomposed as: sign(result) = sign(A) XOR sign(B) and |result| = |A| × |B|. The sign is determined by a single XOR gate — the simplest quantum gate possible. The magnitude is computed by a separate quantum circuit operating only on the magnitude bits (all bits except the sign bit), which are all non-negative. Both parts run in the same circuit, simultaneously.

Phase 1 — Sign XOR gate

The sign bits of A and B (their leftmost bits) are each loaded into a single qubit. A CNOT gate (controlled-NOT) implements XOR between them. The result qubit collapses to 0 (positive) or 1 (negative) immediately — this phase takes constant time, independent of n.

Sign XOR examples
+A (sign=0) ⊕ +B (sign=0) = 0 → Result POSITIVE
+A (sign=0) ⊕ −B (sign=1) = 1 → Result NEGATIVE
−A (sign=1) ⊕ +B (sign=0) = 1 → Result NEGATIVE
−A (sign=1) ⊕ −B (sign=1) = 0 → Result POSITIVE
Phase 2 — Magnitude parallel circuit
1
Encode magnitudes
Strip the sign bits. Load |A| and |B| (the remaining n−1 bits each) into separate qubit registers. These are always non-negative.
2
Hadamard superposition on |B| register
Each qubit of the |B| register passes through a Hadamard (H) gate simultaneously, placing the entire register into superposition of all 2n−1 values at once — all partial product conditions evaluated in parallel.
3
Controlled-phase gates
CPHASE or Toffoli gates entangle each qubit of |B| with a shifted copy of |A|, encoding every partial product of |A|×|B| into the quantum phase without collapsing the superposition.
4
Quantum Fourier Transform + measurement
QFT applies constructive interference on the correct product state and destructive interference on all wrong answers. Measurement collapses the register to |A|×|B| with very high probability.
5
Apply sign to magnitude
If the XOR result (sign qubit) is 1, negate the magnitude result using a quantum two's complement circuit. Prepend the sign bit. The final 2n-bit two's complement product is read out.
Performance
Circuit depth
O(log n)
Doubling the bit width adds only one extra circuit layer. The gap over classical O(n) grows exponentially with n.
Sign handling
Single XOR gate
The sign is resolved by one CNOT gate in constant time — no iteration, no subtraction needed. Cleaner than Booth's for the sign step.
Output type
Probabilistic
Result is correct with high probability, not certainty. Quantum error correction and repeated measurements increase confidence.
Hardware
QPU required
Requires a quantum processor with low decoherence and high gate fidelity. Not practical for small inputs today — advantage emerges at large n.
Important: The "2 cycles" shown in the simulator — initialisation and measurement — is a simplified pedagogical model. A real quantum signed multiplier for two 4-bit numbers would require dozens of physical gates and tens of physical qubits after error correction overhead. Quantum advantage over Booth's algorithm is a near-future milestone, not a present reality.

Classical vs Quantum — Signed Multiplication
Both approaches produce the same signed product. The difference is entirely in how they handle the sign and how they scale with input size.
Side-by-side comparison
Classical — Booth's
  • Sign handled implicitly by subtract step
  • Inspects one bit pair per cycle
  • Arithmetic right shift propagates sign
  • Exactly n cycles for n-bit input
  • Sequential — each cycle depends on last
  • Always exact — deterministic
  • Runs on any CPU today
  • No error correction needed
Quantum — Sign-Magnitude
  • Sign handled by explicit XOR gate
  • All bit pairs evaluated simultaneously
  • Sign applied via two's complement circuit
  • O(log n) circuit layers regardless of n
  • Parallel — no sequential dependency
  • Probabilistic — high but not certain
  • Requires a quantum processor (QPU)
  • Needs quantum error correction

Key trade-offs
Classical strength
Reliability today
Exact result every time on hardware that already exists. For inputs under thousands of bits, classical is always faster in practice.
Quantum strength
Scalability at large n
At very large bit widths (cryptographic key sizes), the logarithmic depth gives exponential advantage — making quantum computing a threat to RSA encryption.
Classical weakness
Linear scaling
Every extra bit adds exactly one more cycle. At 2048-bit key sizes, even optimised classical multipliers are computationally heavy.
Quantum weakness
Hardware immaturity
Decoherence, gate error rates, and qubit connectivity constraints make the physical overhead far larger than the theoretical circuit depth suggests.
Bottom line: Use Booth's algorithm for all signed multiplication today. Study the quantum approach to understand where large-scale integer arithmetic is heading — particularly in post-quantum cryptography and fault-tolerant quantum computing research.
